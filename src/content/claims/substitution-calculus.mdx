If you watch a football match, you'll notice a strange pattern: regardless of the scoreline, the first substitution is almost always made between the 65th and 70th minute. This is not a coincidence; it is a mathematical calculation of biological output.

## The Intersection of Output Curves

Over 90 minutes, a starting player's physical output (sprint speed, recovery time, pressing intensity) follows an **Exponential Decay Curve**. By the 65th minute, their lactic acid buildup forces their high-intensity output to drop below 60% of their baseline.

Conversely, when a substitute is brought onto the pitch, their output does not start at zero. It acts as a **Step Function**—an immediate, vertical spike in intensity back up to near 100%. 

## The Break-Even Point

If a manager subs a player too early (e.g., at halftime), they waste the remaining high-output minutes of the starter, and the substitute will also succumb to exponential decay before the final whistle. If a manager waits too long (e.g., the 85th minute), the starter becomes a massive defensive liability due to fatigue.

The mathematical "break-even" point—where the starter's decaying output drops dangerously below the tactical threshold, but late enough that the substitute can maintain peak intensity until the final whistle—is the **68th minute**.
